Answers for questions

1 Why do most companies use cloud-based email and calendar services?

Companies use services like Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 because running your own mail server used to mean buying hardware, hiring IT staff to maintain it, applying security patches, and handling backups all by yourself. A cloud provider takes over all of that. The company just pays a monthly fee per user and gets email, a shared calendar, video calls, and document storage that all work together and can be accessed from any device.

The main reasons are lower cost (no need for an in-house team just to keep email running), better reliability (large providers run huge, highly available data centers with strong uptime guarantees), stronger security (built-in spam filtering, phishing protection, and two-factor authentication), and easier collaboration (shared calendars and real-time document editing across a team).

For example, a small company with 20 employees would need a dedicated IT person just to run its own Exchange server, while the same company can subscribe to Microsoft 365 for a fixed monthly cost and get all of that managed automatically.

2 What would a mid-size company store in Amazon Glacier, and what not?

Amazon Glacier is designed specifically for archiving data that is accessed very rarely. It is extremely cheap per gigabyte, but retrieving the data back can take anywhere from minutes to several hours, depending on the retrieval option chosen.

A company would store things like old financial records it is legally required to keep for 7-10 years but never actually opens, database backups kept only for disaster-recovery purposes, old system logs, or historical data that must be retained but is not actively used.

A company would not store active, frequently accessed data there - such as the current production database or files employees open daily - because Glacier's slow retrieval time makes it impractical for anything that needs to be available quickly.

Other large cloud backup/archive providers include Azure Archive Storage, Google Cloud Storage (Coldline/Archive tier), and Backblaze B2.

3 Well-known Amazon S3 data leak incidents

Verizon (2017): A partner company set up an S3 bucket without proper security, exposing personal data of about 6 million Verizon customers, including names, addresses, and account PINs.

Voter database leak (2017): A political data analytics firm misconfigured an S3 bucket, exposing personal information of nearly 200 million US voters.

Accenture (2017): A misconfigured bucket exposed master access keys for Accenture's AWS account, meaning an attacker could potentially have taken control of much more than just the leaked files.

What went wrong in all these cases was fundamentally the same: the bucket's access permission was set to allow public/anonymous read access, usually by mistake or because the default settings were not reviewed carefully.

Automated search tools (do not use - illegal): Tools exist that scan the internet for public S3 buckets by guessing common naming patterns (like companyname-backup or companyname-data), similar to how the search engine Shodan scans for exposed devices. Their existence highlights why bucket permissions must be checked carefully, not why they are safe to use.

4 Comparing cloud storage providers (home users)

Comparing Google One, Dropbox, and OneDrive for personal use: Google Drive offers the most generous free tier at 15 GB, compared to OneDrive's 5 GB and Dropbox's 2 GB, though that 15 GB is shared across Gmail and Google Photos too. On paid plans, Google One is generally the cheapest entry point, and at the popular 2 TB tier Google One costs $9.99/month versus Dropbox's $11.99/month for the same storage. OneDrive is rarely bought as a standalone product for home users - it usually comes bundled with a Microsoft 365 subscription, which also includes desktop Office apps, making it a good deal if you already use Word and Excel.

For a student or home user who is already invested in one ecosystem (Gmail vs. Outlook), the existing ecosystem usually matters more than the small price differences between providers.

5 Comparing web hosting providers (not CDN)

Classic web hosting providers - as opposed to a PaaS platform like Rahti - rent out server space for a website, typically with a control panel (like cPanel), a free domain, and a basic set of tools included.

Hostinger is known for very low starting prices and is popular with students and small personal sites, though support and performance scale down at the cheapest tiers. Bluehost is officially recommended by WordPress and integrates closely with it, making it a common choice for blogs, but it is typically pricier at renewal than the introductory price suggests. GoDaddy is one of the largest and most recognizable hosting brands, offering domains and hosting together, but is often criticized for aggressive upselling during checkout.

Most of these providers offer tiers ranging from basic shared hosting (cheapest, but your site shares server resources with many others) up to VPS or dedicated hosting (more expensive, but with dedicated resources) - a company choosing between them should think about expected traffic, not just the advertised starting price, since shared hosting can slow down significantly once a site grows.
